Buying a VIX-linked product is not the same as buying the VIX.

The VIX Index is an option-derived measure of expected 30-day S&P 500 volatility. It cannot be held directly. What investors actually trade are instruments linked to it, including VIX futures, options and exchange-traded products.

That distinction changes the return.

A VIX future prices a future settlement, not today's VIX level. A typical short-term VIX futures index then holds the first two maturities and rolls exposure every day. Its result depends on:

• movements in the futures themselves
• convergence toward settlement
• the shape of the futures curve
• collateral return, fees and product design

When the curve is in contango, longer-dated futures trade above nearer contracts. Cboe reported that this structure appeared more than 80% of the time from 2010 through July 2022. That is historical evidence for that sample, not a rule for every regime.

The latest S&P Dow Jones Indices dashboard offers a useful snapshot. As of 16 September 2026, the S&P 500 VIX Short-Term Futures Index had total returns of -14.17% over one month, -30.08% YTD and -46.14% over 12 months.

Those figures do not mean long volatility always loses. In a volatility shock, backwardation and fast futures repricing can reverse the result sharply. They do show why a correct view on spot VIX can still produce a disappointing trade.

Before using a VIX-linked product, ask:

1. Am I looking at the index, a future or a rolling product?
2. What does the current futures curve look like?
3. How many days remain to settlement or rebalance?
4. Does the product use leverage or a daily reset?
5. What happens if volatility spikes before liquidity improves?

The underlying idea may be right while the instrument is wrong.

Which matters more in your process: the VIX level or the futures curve?
